I use analytical tools to tackle open questions in biology. After an M.S. in Physics, where I worked on transport models, and a PhD in Applied Mathematics on modeling cancer relapse, I became fascinated by how evolution and randomness have shaped, and continue to shape, the history of life. My current research interests are:
- Survival in fluctuating environments. Through bet-hedging, populations spread risk by randomly switching individuals between survival strategies. I study whether bet-hedging fits a general framework, the role of nonlinearity, and how the timing of decisions affects fitness.
- Dormancy. Many cells survive harsh conditions by partially shutting down their metabolism, a strategy that may date back to the earliest forms of life. I study how cells enter and exit dormancy, how metabolic activity can be tuned to maximize fitness, and whether trait suppression was among the first ways life coped with adversity.
- Periodic natural rhythms. From circadian clocks to seasonal cycles, many organisms anticipate regular changes in their environment. I study how internal rhythms emerge from gene regulation and how they help organisms survive in periodic environments.
- Noise in gene expression. I explore how the cell cycle and molecular noise create differences between genetically identical cells, and whether suppressing gene expression can improve survival.
I like to work where chance meets selection, an interplay that two sharp thinkers summarized better than I ever could:

“Mutation is random; natural selection is the very opposite of random.”
— Richard Dawkins

“Natural selection is a mechanism for generating an exceedingly high degree of improbability.”
— Ronald A. Fisher
